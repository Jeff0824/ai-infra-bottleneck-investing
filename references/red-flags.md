# Red flags and failure modes

Check every conclusion against these before publishing.

## In the source material

- **Self-reported returns.** Unaudited, self-published performance figures are marketing, not a track record. Report them as claims.
- **Concentration masquerading as conviction.** A book that swings 100+ percentage points in a month on sector sentiment is leveraged beta, whatever the framing.
- **Anecdote as evidence.** Executive quotes and viral posts are sentiment data, not demand data. Label them.
- **Single-thesis exposure.** If every position depends on one variable (e.g. "compute demand is insatiable"), the portfolio has one risk, not several.
- **Gated positions.** If the source withholds allocation weights behind a paywall, you cannot verify the thesis was actually held as described.

## In the analysis itself

- **"It has a floor" when it does not.** A low forward multiple is a floor on the *multiple*, not on the *price*. Cyclical earnings can collapse into a cheap multiple.
- **Citing the downside case approvingly.** Quoting "historically these fell ~49% peak-to-trough" in a section arguing the sector is low-risk is a contradiction, not support.
- **Unfalsifiable forecasts.** Back-of-envelope ARR projections built on one executive's comment are scenarios, not estimates. Say so.
- **No sizing, no stops, no invalidation.** A thesis without a size and an exit rule is a story.
- **Behaviour contradicts stated process.** If the author says they sold near the top and re-bought too early, the drawdown was caused by the plan, not by bad luck.

## Disclosure

State clearly when the analysis is derived from a monetised source (paid newsletter, Discord alerts). The incentive to publish conviction is structural, regardless of the author's integrity.
